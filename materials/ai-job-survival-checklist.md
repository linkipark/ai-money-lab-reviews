# The AI Job-Survival Checklist

*From AI Money Lab — the companion to "I Tested AI on the 5 Jobs Americans Fear Losing Most."*

One rule behind everything here: **AI won't take your job, but someone using AI will.** Every item below is a concrete move you can start this week — no hype, no "learn to code," no fabricated statistics.

---

## 1. Customer Service Representatives

**Where you stand:** the AI already answers the routine questions. Your edge is the conversation AI handles badly — the angry, the confused, the one with three problems tangled together.

### Survival actions
1. **Become the escalation handler.** Let the bot (or a bot-using colleague) take tier-1 tickets. Position yourself for the complex, emotional, high-stakes cases — refunds with judgment calls, policy edge cases, customers who need to feel heard.
2. **Learn to train the bot.** Someone configures the knowledge base, reviews AI answers, and decides when the bot escalates. That role is called an AI conversation designer or bot supervisor — and it pays better than rep work. You already know what customers ask; that's the hardest part of the job.
3. **Document your resolutions.** Write down the unusual fixes, the exceptions, the "I called the warehouse and did X." AI-readable knowledge bases are what companies buy. The person who wrote the good answers becomes indispensable.
4. **Specialize in one product line deeply.** The bot knows the manual; you know the workarounds. Specialists survive longer than generalists in support.

### Prompts to use this week
- `"A customer says: [paste ticket]. Draft three response versions — apologetic, firm, and brief. Tell me which fits best and why."`
- `"Here are 20 of my resolved tickets [paste or attach]. What are the top 5 recurring issues, and what FAQ answers would deflect them?"`
- `"Roleplay as an angry customer with [problem]. I need to practice. Push back twice before accepting my solution, and rate my tone at the end."`

## 2. Data Entry Keyers

**Where you stand:** typing data from one place to another is the exact thing AI is cheapest at. Your way up is owning *accuracy* — AI makes errors confidently, and companies still need a human signature on the numbers.

### Survival actions
1. **Become the verifier, not the typer.** Offer your boss a deal: "Let AI do first-pass entry; I'll audit the exceptions and sign off." The audit role survives because accountability does.
2. **Learn the automation you fear.** Prompting, spreadsheet formulas, and simple RPA tools (even macros) are what the AI uses. Learn enough to be the person who *runs* the pipeline instead of being replaced by it.
3. **Move into data quality.** Dirty data costs companies real money. Learn to spot duplicates, reconcile sources, and write validation rules — "data quality analyst" is a real title that grows while "keyer" shrinks.
4. **Own the source systems.** The person who knows *why* the invoices arrive late, which vendor's PDFs break the parser, and who to call — that context never gets automated.

### Prompts to use this week
- `"I'm migrating [type of data, e.g. invoices] from [source] to [system]. Give me a validation checklist of the 10 most common errors in this kind of migration."`
- `"Here are two records that should match [paste]. List every discrepancy and write the SQL/Excel formula to flag these automatically."`
- `"Explain what 'data quality analyst' involves day to day, and the 3 cheapest ways to build those skills without a degree."`

## 3. Computer Programmers

**Where you stand:** AI writes boilerplate fast — that's now the cheapest commodity in tech. What still sells: knowing what to build, spotting what's wrong, and owning systems nobody else understands.

### Survival actions
1. **Become the reviewer, not the typist.** AI generates; humans approve. Get genuinely good at code review — security, architecture, edge cases. Junior-level coding is automated; judgment about code is not.
2. **Spec and test like your job depends on it — it does.** The highest-value skill in an AI-assisted workflow is writing precise requirements and rigorous tests. AI produces code; you define "correct."
3. **Go where AI is weakest:** legacy systems, security, compliance, regulated industries. AI has thin training data on 20-year-old COBOL-era systems and real security incident response — and nobody lets a bot own those alone.
4. **Ship portfolio proof.** Keep a small repo of things you built *with* AI, fast. "I ship production features in days, with tests" beats any résumé line in 2026.

### Prompts to use this week
- `"Review this code like a senior engineer: [paste]. Flag security issues, logic errors, and performance problems. Be strict."`
- `"Write comprehensive unit tests for [function/module]. Cover edge cases, invalid inputs, and failure modes — I want to see what good test discipline looks like."`
- `"This legacy code does [X]. Explain what it does in plain English, then suggest the safest minimal refactor: [paste]."`

## 4. Bookkeeping / Accounting Clerks

**Where you stand:** reconciliation, categorization, invoice entry — all automatable. What isn't: explaining numbers to a worried owner, spotting fraud, and making judgment calls where the rules are gray.

### Survival actions
1. **Move from recording to advising.** "Your margins dropped 4% — here's why" is a different job from "I entered the invoices." Owners pay for interpretation, not input.
2. **Own the month-end close.** AI drafts the close checklist; the human signs it, explains variances, and answers the auditor. Be the signer.
3. **Specialize in the messy stuff:** sales tax across states, contractor vs. employee classification, audit prep. Rule-gray areas need human accountability.
4. **Learn to supervise the accounting AI.** Tools like Dext, Ramp, and modern QuickBooks now auto-categorize. The clerk who sets the rules and reviews the exceptions becomes the indispensable one.

### Prompts to use this week
- `"I have a bank reconciliation with these discrepancies [paste]. Walk me through the most likely causes, in order of probability."`
- `"Draft a plain-English email to a small business owner explaining why their Q3 profit looks lower than their cash balance. No jargon."`
- `"Give me a month-end close checklist for a small business with payroll, inventory, and 3 revenue streams — including what to double-check that AI tools commonly get wrong."`

## 5. Telemarketers / Telephone Operators

**Where you stand:** cold volume calling is AI's easiest win. Trust-building conversations — discovery, demos, negotiation — are still human work, and that's where you move.

### Survival actions
1. **Move up the call: warm, not cold.** Cold outreach scripts get automated first. Discovery calls, demos, and closing conversations need a human reading the room.
2. **Sell the skill behind the call.** Objection handling, reading tone, negotiating — these transfer to account management, customer success, and B2B sales roles that pay more than dialing.
3. **Learn to run the AI calling campaigns.** Companies still need humans to write the scripts, set the targeting, review transcripts, and intervene when calls go wrong. The campaign operator replaces the dialer.
4. **Record your wins as transcripts.** One folder of your best calls — hard objection overcome, angry prospect saved — is proof no résumé line can match.

### Prompts to use this week
- `"Write a 30-second cold outreach opener for [product/service, target buyer]. Give me 3 versions: casual, data-driven, and curiosity-based."`
- `"Roleplay as a skeptical [buyer title]. Give me your three toughest objections about [product]. After I answer, critique my responses."`
- `"Here are notes from a call that didn't close [paste]. What went wrong, and what should I have asked differently?"`

## Universal Moves (every job, every week)

These two work no matter which of the five jobs you hold:

1. **Become the AI supervisor, not the AI competitor.** In every role above, there's a version of your job that directs the AI — reviewer, auditor, trainer, campaign operator. That's the promotion path; the doing-it-by-hand path shrinks.
2. **Learn the adjacent judgment skill.** The rep learns escalation; the keyer learns data quality; the programmer learns review and spec; the clerk learns advising; the caller learns closing. In every case: move one step toward *judgment*, one step away from *repetition*.
3. **Document your judgment.** Playbooks, exception logs, decision records — written human judgment becomes training material, and the author becomes the person the company keeps.
4. **Build one piece of proof.** A repo, a portfolio of wins, a case study, a before/after — AI users win by *showing* what they ship with AI, not by listing what they did without it.
